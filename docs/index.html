What you are measuring

A polarized target has unequal populations of nuclear spin states. Its NMR spectrum contains information about that imbalance. We train a network to map a measured voltage sweep to the vector polarization, P.

For a spin-1 system, P = n+ − n−, where the populations sum to one. Our labels are fractions: P = 0.05 means 5% polarization.

Who this is for

You know basic Python, arrays, and introductory physics. You can start without prior neural-network experience. The reading and coding labs are self-contained.

All three learning phases concern vector extraction. The simulator uses a theoretical spin-1 Dulya/Pake powder doublet. Instrument settings and parameter ranges are teaching assumptions; matching them to measurements remains a separate scientific requirement.

01

Phase one · Data generation

A network can only learn the experiment you give it.

The generator is a model of the measurement process. Its job is to produce realistic spectra with known answers, including the variations a trained model will encounter later.

V(f) = S(f; P, θline, cc) × G(f; θgain) + B(f; θcircuit) + ε(f)

Here S is the spin-dependent lineshape, G is the instrument gain, B is the baseline, and ε is noise. The symbols θ collect extra parameters—widths, centers, tuning, and so on—that affect a spectrum even when P stays the same. These are often called nuisance parameters.

Physical requirement: the Q-curve is the detector response with the nuclear susceptibility set to zero. The signal is the change in that response when susceptibility is included. Coil impedance, tuning capacitance, cable transformation, electrical loading, and detector phase determine the voltage. Phase also affects the signal; an independently sampled straight-line gain is not a full replacement for that response. See §2.1 and Fig. 1 of the paper and the equation-by-equation notes.

Why a Pake doublet is not two Gaussian peaks

A powder contains many quadrupolar-axis orientations. Each spin-1 transition family therefore contributes an extended, asymmetric branch. In the axial, zero-broadening limit, one branch extends from normalized frequency −2 to +1 with a horn at +1; the other is its reflection. Their sum has horns near ±1, outer shoulders near ±2, and signal between the horns.

tools/lineshape.py evaluates the supplied Dulya branch formula, including Lorentzian broadening and a weighted azimuthal average. We use x = (f − center_mhz) / split_mhz, quadrupole asymmetry eta, and dimensionless broadening g. The physical Lorentzian half-width is g × split_mhz; split_mhz is approximately the center-to-horn distance, not the full doublet separation.

Spin-temperature populations set the two transition weights. In the weak-quadrupole approximation, their ratio is r = (√(4 − 3P²) + P) / (2 − 2P). The code uses an equivalent expression that stays finite at P = 0 and ±1. Negative polarization reverses the signal and exchanges the stronger horn; the negative calibration in this example makes positive-P absorption point downward.

Before gain and noise, the discrete calibration convention is sum(S) = P / cc on this 512-bin grid. It is not a frequency integral. This is a single-site, spin-temperature model; independent tensor polarization, multiple sites, and a full circuit response are outside this lab. See Dulya et al., A line-shape analysis for spin-1 NMR signals (1997) for the underlying theory.

1. Match the generator to experimental data

Begin with representative real scans across acquisition periods, tuning conditions, and polarization values. Check the frequency grid, voltage units, baseline convention, and calibration. Fit the circuit response and spectral shape, then inspect overlays and residuals. A visually plausible average trace does not establish that the generator covers the real experiment.

The current lab code combines the theoretical powder doublet in tools/lineshape.py with a linear gain curve and cubic baseline in tools/nmr_lab.py, over 512 bins from 32.3 to 33.1 MHz. This is an unvalidated prototype, not the generator described in the paper. Its settings—including split_mhz = 0.080, g = 0.080, and eta = 0.030—do not establish experimental matching.

The required next implementation is the physical circuit model, with explicit units and detection channel, followed by checks against representative measured baselines and spectra. Only then should supported joint parameter distributions be used for training. The existing --configurations schema still describes the prototype; inserting fitted values does not turn it into a physical circuit model.

Before generating thousands of events

  • Compare peak positions, widths, asymmetry, amplitudes, and baseline curvature.
  • Inspect fit residuals against frequency and operating conditions; structured residuals can indicate missing physics.
  • Hold out acquisition periods for later checks. Reusing a scan to tune the simulator and claim final accuracy weakens the claim.

2. Learn the noise, too

Start with baseline-only or repeated scans under stable conditions. Estimate the residual mean and standard deviation, but also inspect autocorrelation, the power spectrum, tails, and how noise changes with frequency or time. For two independent repeats with the same underlying signal, their difference divided by √2 has the single-scan noise variance. Drift or correlated repeats violate that assumption.

Paper convention: Eq. (45) uses max(|signal|) / max(|noise|), excluding the baseline. A peak-signal/noise-SD ratio is different. The current Gaussian/AR(1) settings are demonstrations, not the paper's measured noise covariance, and their voltage scale has not been reconciled with it.

What is implemented

The default is independent, zero-mean Gaussian noise with one standard deviation per generation run:

εj ∼ Normal(0, σnoise²)

--noise-level 2.7e-5 is a teaching setting in the generator’s voltage units. It is not a measured experimental constant. The optional --noise-correlation enables an AR(1) example with the same marginal variance.

What students should investigate

If residuals show drift, interference, or heavy tails, Gaussian models may miss them. Compare measured and simulated residual distributions and correlations before extending simulate().

Estimate noise settings using development data. Keep the experimental evaluation set separate. When reporting SNR, state your convention, such as peak absolute signal divided by noise standard deviation.

Small Python experiment: equal noise width, different correlation

This standalone teaching example produces two noise models with similar marginal standard deviation.

import numpy as np
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt

rng = np.random.default_rng(42)
sigma, rho = 2.7e-5, 0.85
white = rng.normal(0, sigma, 512)
correlated = np.empty(512)
correlated[0] = rng.normal(0, sigma)
for j in range(1, 512):
    correlated[j] = (rho * correlated[j-1]
                     + rng.normal(0, sigma * np.sqrt(1-rho**2)))
for name, noise in [('white', white), ('correlated', correlated)]:
    lag1 = np.corrcoef(noise[:-1], noise[1:])[0, 1]
    print(name, 'SD:', noise.std(), 'lag-1 correlation:', lag1)
    plt.plot(noise, label=name, alpha=0.8)
plt.xlabel('Frequency bin')
plt.ylabel('Noise voltage')
plt.legend()
plt.show()

Explain why matching only the histogram or SNR cannot distinguish these cases. The AR(1) process above is an illustration, not a claim about a particular instrument.

3. Vary parameters within physical constraints

Audit every encoded parameter: what is known, what varies, what is fixed, and which parameters are correlated? Robust training needs coverage of plausible combinations. Independently randomizing every knob can create impossible spectra and destroy real correlations.

A useful design check

More variability can reveal missing information.

The leading amplitude scale contains P / cc. If calibration varies, amplitude alone cannot tell you which factor changed. The tutorial records the known cc and uses it to scale the detrended input. In an experiment, obtain calibration independently; do not infer it from the polarization label you are trying to predict.

Start with a small pipeline check. Then increase both the number of noise realizations and the number of distinct physical configurations. A million spectra generated from a handful of configurations are not a million independent examples of experimental variability.

02

Phase two · Network design

Enough capacity to learn.
Small enough to use.

Choose the simplest model that captures the relevant spectral structure and meets your measured inference-time budget. Parameter count alone tells you neither.

A neural network is a sequence of adjustable transformations. Training chooses its weights so that predicted P agrees with known labels. A deep neural network (DNN) has multiple layers; a convolutional neural network (CNN) is a DNN whose filters share weights across positions.

For a one-dimensional voltage sweep, a 1D CNN is a natural candidate. Nearby bins jointly describe peaks and shoulders, while broader patterns describe the baseline. Shared filters can detect a feature in different parts of a sweep. This helps with shifts, but does not make the full estimator perfectly shift-invariant.

Inside the tutorial networks

Code your first small 1D CNN

This standalone example shows the essential PyTorch structure. __init__ defines the layers; forward defines how a batch flows through them. Two input channels correspond to raw and detrended spectra. Start here before reading the larger multiscale model.

import torch
from torch import nn

class SmallPolarizationCNN(nn.Module):
    def __init__(self):
        super().__init__()
        self.encoder = nn.Sequential(
            nn.Conv1d(2, 16, kernel_size=7, padding=3),
            nn.GELU(),
            nn.AvgPool1d(2),
            nn.Conv1d(16, 32, kernel_size=7, padding=3),
            nn.GELU(),
            nn.AdaptiveAvgPool1d(1),
        )
        self.head = nn.Linear(32, 1)

    def forward(self, x):
        features = self.encoder(x).squeeze(-1)
        return self.head(features)

model = SmallPolarizationCNN()
example_batch = torch.randn(8, 2, 512)
print(model(example_batch).shape)  # torch.Size([8, 1])
print(sum(p.numel() for p in model.parameters()))

The numbers are untrained outputs, not polarization estimates you can use. This same model is available in the trainer as --architecture compact. Both teaching networks are defined in tools/nmr_lab.py. To add another architecture, extend build_model() and the CLI choices, then compare validation error and latency with the same data and split.

The default PhysicsMultiscaleCNN combines learned filters with spectral summaries. Preprocessing builds a raw channel and a residual channel after subtracting a cubic fit to the outer wings. The code selects |u| ≥ 0.65, where u = (f − 32.7) / 0.4 spans −1 to +1 across the whole sweep: about 17.5% at each end. This preprocessing coordinate is different from the lineshape coordinate x = (f − center_mhz) / split_mhz. Known cc multiplies the residual before standardization.

Different filter widths see different spectral scales. Dilation expands the context without proportionally increasing weights. Pooling compresses the frequency axis before the dense head. The physics estimate gives the CNN a useful starting point, and the correction head learns what the summaries miss.

Keep physical information

Feature means and standard deviations are fitted on the training partition and reused later. Independently rescaling every spectrum to unit amplitude could erase the amplitude information needed to recover polarization.

Keep inference measurable

The multiscale teaching model has 82,391 parameters; the compact CNN has 3,889. These counts include all weights, including frozen ones. Neither count is a latency measurement.

Lab 02 · Measure the cost of your architecture

Decide the required response time and target hardware first. Measure batch size one for a live scan, and a larger batch for offline throughput. The helper uses PyTorch’s benchmark timer, evaluation mode, and disabled gradient tracking.

python tools/benchmark_network.py \
  --architecture physics_multiscale --device cpu --batch-size 1
python tools/benchmark_network.py \
  --architecture compact --device cpu --batch-size 1

The helper times randomly initialized versions of the exact architectures. It measures forward-pass cost, not accuracy. It excludes file loading, baseline preprocessing, transfers, and startup; measure those too before making a deployment claim. Divide batch time by batch size to report throughput cost per event, not single-event latency. See the PyTorch benchmarking guide.

When calibration varies between events

The dataset always stores a known cc for each event. Both networks use make_features(), which multiplies the detrended residual by that calibration before training-set standardization. The prediction script applies the same operation. Supply different cc values through a configuration JSON list to study variation.

Obtain calibration independently in an experiment. This preprocessing convention is part of the tutorial's model assumptions; it does not establish that every instrument response can be normalized this way.

03

Phase three · Training & evaluation

Learn when to stop.
Then learn what the errors mean.

Training reduces a loss. Validation chooses a model. A held-out evaluation measures how that choice generalizes. These are different jobs.

Split by the source of variation

Several simulated spectra can share the same parameter configuration. If those near-relatives appear in both training and test data, a random row split can give an optimistic estimate of performance on unfamiliar configurations.

80%Train weights & scalers
10%Choose checkpoint
10%Evaluate

The tutorial trainer always groups by configuration_id, keeping each configuration entirely in one partition. The 80/10/10 proportions apply to groups; row proportions can differ. Check split.json. For stronger tests, also hold out acquisition periods or parameter families. A group split cannot test a missing noise process or repair an unrealistic simulator.

Match the training objective to the scientific target

L = mean [ (P̂ − P) / strain ]²

The trainer uses mean squared error normalized by the training-label standard deviation strain. For a single P output, this constant rescaling preserves the absolute-MSE objective. A relative-error loss would weight small polarizations more strongly, which can conflict with an absolute-error target. Keep the training objective and the scientific acceptance criterion aligned.

Lab 03 · Train on the dataset from Lab 01

python tools/train_model.py \
  --data local-results/smoke.npz \
  --output-dir local-results/smoke_model \
  --architecture physics_multiscale --epochs 20 --patience 8 \
  --batch-size 128 --learning-rate 0.0001 --seed 42

This small run checks the workflow. For a larger pilot, increase the dataset and epoch cap. Change one factor at a time: use --architecture compact with the same data and seed for an architecture comparison. The trainer selects CUDA when available; pass --device cpu for a CPU-only run.

Train long enough without chasing the training set

An epoch is one pass through the training data. A batch is the subset used for one optimizer update. The learning rate controls the step size. Our trainer uses AdamW with weight decay, dropout in the correction head, and a scheduler that reduces the learning rate when validation loss stalls.

Track both curves. Falling training loss with persistently rising validation loss suggests overfitting. If both remain high, consider underfitting, optimization trouble, or missing information. Early stopping limits training after a specified number of epochs without a new best validation value; it does not prove the model is correct. The trainer restores and saves the best validation checkpoint, not simply the last epoch.

Read the curves in context

The multiscale model uses dropout during training and disables it for validation, so training loss can exceed validation loss. The compact example does not use dropout. For the single-output absolute MSE used here, sqrt(val_loss) × target_scale converts validation loss back to RMSE in fractional P.

The trainer saves model.pt, scaler.npz, config.json, split.json, history.csv, metrics.json, and test_predictions.csv. The prediction CSV retains configuration IDs for group-based analysis. Keep these files together. This version saves at the end of a completed run; an interrupted run may leave no checkpoint.

Accuracy and precision need separate numbers

Define the residual as prediction minus truth: ei = P̂i − Pi. Bias measures a systematic offset; width measures the spread around that offset. In this tutorial we use bias to quantify systematic accuracy and residual width to quantify precision. Accuracy more broadly concerns closeness to truth, so also report total error.

The paper's Eq. (38) uses truth minus prediction, the opposite sign. Reverse the bias sign when comparing; width and RMSE do not change. Paper tables use polarization percent, whereas the saved tutorial metrics use fractional P.

Bias · systematic offset
b = mean(e)

A positive value means the model overestimates P on average.

Width · precision
σ = √mean[(e − b)²]

Residual standard deviation, not the standard error of the mean.

RMSE · total error
R = √mean(e²)

Combines offset and spread. It can be large for either reason.

RMSE² = bias² + width²

This empirical identity is exact when width uses ddof=0, as the tutorial helper does. It does not require Gaussian residuals. Inspect a histogram, the 95th percentile of absolute errors, and performance by polarization and noise level: one width can hide outliers and systematic trends.

Put the numbers on a polarization scale

Absolute metrics are in fractional P. Multiply by 100 to obtain percentage points (pp). To express an error relative to a chosen nonzero reference P₀, divide the fractional error by |P₀| and multiply by 100.

0.0005 fractional P = 0.05 pp
100 × 0.0005 / 0.05 = 1% relative error at P₀ = 5%

The same absolute error is 5% relative at P₀ = 1%, and 0.25% relative at P₀ = 20%. Relative error is undefined at zero. A denominator floor can stabilize a diagnostic, but the result depends on that convention.

Think it through: can a narrow distribution still be inaccurate?

Yes. Set width to 0.010 pp and bias to 0.100 pp. Predictions cluster tightly, but far from truth. Conversely, zero bias with a broad width gives an unbiased but imprecise estimator. To reach the RMSE target, the combination of both contributions must fit the error budget.

Measure performance near the polarization you care about

Converting a pooled RMSE to “relative error at 5%” is only a change of scale. To claim actual performance near 5%, select held-out events in a stated signed P band—for example, 0.045 ≤ P < 0.055—then recompute bias, width, and RMSE. Positive and negative P can have different biases, so inspect them separately before combining signs.

python tools/analyze_predictions.py \
  local-results/smoke_model/test_predictions.csv \
  --p0 0.05 --half-width 0.005

The helper reports pooled and local-band metrics, sample counts, MAE, and the 95th-percentile absolute error. A tiny band may contain too few events in a smoke run; an empty band returns null metrics, not zero error. Width is not a calibrated uncertainty interval for an individual prediction. For error bars on your reported metrics, resample independent configurations or use repeated independent evaluations; treating related rows as independent understates uncertainty.

Interpret the comparisons carefully

The narrow-variation RMSE corresponds to about 2.56% of a 5% reference polarization. That pooled conversion alone does not establish 2.56% performance at P = 5%; the local-band result above answers a different question.

The two broad-variation runs use identical data and grouped partitions, so they compare the models under the recorded training settings. The compact CNN is cheaper but less accurate in this example. This does not prove that every compact architecture must fail, or that the chosen optimizer settings are optimal for either model.

Keep the claim honest

These examples use a theoretical Pake lineshape with simplified instrument settings and a maximum of 25 training epochs. The narrow and broad datasets have different parameter distributions. A small error on either would not establish experimental performance.

None of these runs meets the example 0.0005 fractional-P RMSE target. Repeatedly consulting test scores can influence design choices; freeze the final method and reserve a fresh evaluation before claiming generalization. Real measurements also require a matched simulator and independently established reference labels.

Bring the three phases together

Your investigation.

Make one defensible improvement and explain the evidence.

  1. Write your measurement contract. State the P range, known calibration, expected noise and drift, absolute-error target, reference polarization, and latency budget.
  2. Audit the generator. Identify what would need matching to data, what varies, and what is still assumed. Compare at least two noise diagnostics.
  3. Run one controlled comparison. Compare the two CNNs on the same dataset and grouped split. Record settings and select using validation results. As a separate stress test, evaluate a fixed model on fresh data with correlated noise.
  4. Build an error budget. Report bias, residual width, RMSE, 95th-percentile absolute error, and event counts—pooled and in a signed P band. Convert to both pp and relative percent.
  5. Check the cost and limits. Report forward-pass and end-to-end timing on your hardware. Explain which unseen conditions your test does and does not cover.
Discussion: does generating ten times more data guarantee improvement?

No. Additional noise realizations help sample a fixed simulator distribution, but do not fix wrong physics, missing calibration, or uncovered configurations. Compare validation performance as you increase independent configuration coverage and event count separately. Keep the final evaluation frozen.

Discussion: how do you tell overfitting from missing information?

A widening training/validation gap suggests a generalization problem. Large errors on both can come from limited capacity, optimization, or an underdetermined input. Controlled tests—fixed calibration, reduced nuisance variation, or simulator-known quantities used only as diagnostics—help separate these explanations.

What you are measuring

A polarized target has unequal populations of nuclear spin states. Its NMR spectrum contains information about that imbalance. We train a network to map a recorded sweep and its corresponding reference to the vector polarization, P.

For a spin-1 system, P = n+ − n−, where the populations sum to one. Our labels are fractions: P = 0.05 means 5% polarization. “Vector polarization” names the physical quantity; the model returns one signed component along the measurement axis.

Who this is for

You know basic Python, arrays, and introductory physics. You can start without prior neural-network experience. The reading and coding labs are self-contained.

The guided path matches a spin-1 Dulya/Pake response and circuit model to supplied measurements. Unmeasured settings and sampled ranges remain explicit assumptions. A separate TE-area benchmark explores a controlled nominal setup.

A simulation-trained polarization estimator

Simulation-based inference means using a forward simulator to learn how observations inform physical parameters. Here we use supervised regression: a neural network learns to return one estimate, P̂, from each spectrum's input channels. Training learns a reusable inverse mapping, so prediction does not run a new nonlinear fit for every sweep. The main walkthrough uses a multiscale convolutional neural network (CNN) with fitted spectral summaries; the lessons also compare simpler dense networks.

Why train on simulations matched to measurements?

Experimental P estimates can carry roughly 5% relative uncertainty in the motivating measurements. They are valuable checks, but cannot serve as exact truth for a much smaller extraction-error claim. The reference paper discusses typical 3–5% relative measurement uncertainties. Read the experimental context.

  1. Constrain the forward model. Use real raw sweeps and known setup information to fit the baseline, lineshape and readout response; inspect residuals and unsupported assumptions.
  2. Generate new labeled sweeps. Choose P, propagate the spin susceptibility through the physical circuit, and sample declared setup variations and noise. P is known because it was an input to that simulation.
  3. Learn and test the inverse. Train on those simulated pairs and evaluate on held-out source groups and conditions before applying the estimator to compatible measurements.

Thus experimental data constrain the simulator and check its realism; fitted experimental P values are not the network's truth labels. Spin populations, susceptibility, the passive cable and detector response constrain generated examples. Exact simulator labels do not make the simulator an exact experiment. The raw/reference walkthrough needs no TE calibration; the separate TE-area benchmark assumes an ideal calibration.

Improve extraction while retaining the experimental error budget

Learning across many physical configurations can reduce errors from noise and competing fit parameters. The reference study reports substantial extraction gains over its conventional fitting comparisons. Those are the paper's results; this tutorial's smaller models require their own comparison on the same held-out inputs before claiming an advantage over fitting. See the study's comparison and scope.

A smaller extraction error is not the total measurement uncertainty

For the systems motivating this tutorial, the stated Q-meter design limit is about 0.8% relative. This figure is supplied as project context. Reducing the network's extraction error below that level does not remove instrument uncertainty. Calibration, drift, coil coupling, field variations, and differences between the sample regions seen by the NMR coil and by the beam can still matter. Simulation-to-experiment mismatch adds another contribution.

At P = 5%, a 0.8% relative scale corresponds to 0.04 percentage points; 5% relative corresponds to 0.25 percentage points. These conversions illustrate units, not achieved network errors. The two uncertainty figures describe different scopes and should not automatically be added together.

Sources and scope of these model and uncertainty statements

The approximately 5% and 0.8% figures were supplied by the repository maintainer as context for the motivating measurements and systems. They are not fitted uncertainties for the included CSVs. The reference paper gives 3–5% for typical measurements and rounds the intrinsic Q-meter scale to about 1% in section 7.5. It does not specify the exact 0.8% figure; that project figure is not assigned a one-standard-deviation convention or a universal hardware limit here.

The physics constraints live in the forward simulator and the selected input representation. Neural training minimizes polarization error; it does not solve a circuit-equation residual at each optimizer update. This is one form of simulation-based point estimation, rather than a claim to implement neural posterior estimation. See the simulation-based inference review and the denoising-autoencoder reference for the broader methods.

Lower spectrum-fit residuals or smaller error bars after denoising do not alone establish smaller true-P errors. 1. Connect spin populations to a real powder lineshape

For spin-temperature populations, tensor polarization is Q = 2 − √(4 − 3P²), and the transition population differences are (P + Q)/2 and (P − Q)/2. The two powder branches come from quadrupolar orientation distributions, with horns near normalized frequency ±1 and shoulders near ±2. They are not isolated Gaussian peaks.

tools/lineshape.py analytically integrates a complex Lorentzian over powder orientations and numerically averages over azimuth. Its real part is dispersion and its negative imaginary part is absorption. Normalization is on the infinite detuning axis: changing the sweep window does not artificially rescale the susceptibility.

Widths, asymmetry, sign, and calibration

Use x = (f − f₀)/split, with frequencies in the same units. The Lorentzian half-width is g × split_mhz; eta is EFG asymmetry, distinct from the coil filling factor. Spin weights remain finite at P = 0 and ±1. This lab models a single quadrupolar site under spin temperature, not independently driven tensor polarization.

The susceptibility amplitude coefficient is an explicit setup parameter. We obtain the area calibration from an independent thermal-equilibrium reference: Ccal = PTE / ∫STE(f)/f df. It does not come from the enhanced spectrum's unknown label. Detector phase and inversion determine voltage polarity.

See Dulya et al. (1997) and the full physics notes.

2. Match the electronics and characterize the noise

The public measured baseline uses the confirmed 500-bin grid: fⱼ = 32.3 + 0.0015287 j MHz, j = 0…499, with nominal center 32.7 MHz. The detector-noise interface accepts a full development-data covariance matrix in V²: ε ∼ Normal(0, Σ). It validates symmetry and positive semidefiniteness. 5. Give difficult polarization regions enough coverage

Uniform polarization sampling is a useful starting point, but it does not make every part of the problem equally easy. Here low polarization means small |P| on either side of zero. At fixed circuit settings, susceptibility scale, linewidth and noise, the nuclear response weakens as |P| decreases. Its two intrinsic branch weights also become more nearly equal, making the small imbalance harder to distinguish from noise and changes in amplitude.

Q ≈ ¾P²,   w₊ = (P + Q)/2,   w₋ = (P − Q)/2
For P ≠ 0: (w₊ − w₋)/(w₊ + w₋) = Q/P ≈ ¾P

This is the small-|P| limit of the spin-temperature model. The magnitude of the intrinsic fractional branch imbalance is only about 0.75% at |P| = 1%. Peak spacing and linewidth do not automatically shrink with P; weaker signal and nearly equal branch weights reduce their visibility. The observed detector peaks also include circuit and phase effects. Extra training examples can improve coverage, but cannot recover information absent from an individual noisy sweep. Use absolute errors near zero, where relative error is undefined.

Inspect validation errors and counts in signed P bands before deciding where to add data. Sample low-|P| examples across the supported noise, linewidth and electronics conditions, preserving their physical relationships. Keep both signs and sufficient coverage at larger |P|. A model trained only near zero is not a broad-range estimator.

For 1,200 training rows, 50% extra allocation within ±5% gives 650 expected rows there, compared with 100 under uniform sampling. At the same total size, coverage outside that band falls from 1,100 to 550.

The broad draws also contribute to the central band, so “50% extra allocation” places more than half of all examples there. This choice is illustrative, not an optimal recipe. A smooth density or stratified draws are other possibilities. Choose the design using development data and evaluate the result on fixed, independent conditions.

New examples, repeated rows, and loss weights have different roles

Generating new low-|P| sweeps adds physical responses and fresh event noise. Repeating an existing row only changes how often the optimizer sees it. Weighting its loss changes the importance of its error. Oversampling and loss weights compound each other if both are applied. Keep these choices explicit and confined to training.

Changing the training P distribution can change the network’s preferred estimate for ambiguous weak spectra. Compare signed bias and error across the full range. If the intended operating population differs from the training mixture, report its own evaluation as well as a per-band diagnostic; a pooled score on the oversampled mixture measures a different objective. The sampling and loss discussion explains the assumptions behind weighting.

Phase two · Network design

Start with an MLP.
Add depth. Learn spectral structure.

Build three estimators, give them the same training data, and measure what each improvement buys you. Our progression moves from a basic dense network to a deeper DNN and a multiscale CNN with physical summaries.

A neural network is a sequence of adjustable transformations. Training chooses its weights so that predicted P agrees with known labels. A multilayer perceptron (MLP) connects each layer densely to the next. Here the first MLP has one hidden layer; the middle model is a deep dense neural network (DNN) with three hidden layers. A convolutional neural network (CNN) is also a DNN, with filters that share weights across frequency positions.

Every model receives the same (batch, 2, 500) input: raw voltage and TE-calibrated, reference-subtracted area contributions. Both channels use standardization fitted on the training rows. This comparison uses the 500-bin TE-area benchmark. The experiment-anchored exercise uses raw/reference recorded units without TE calibration and trains through the same tools.

1. Build a basic MLP

Flatten the two channels into 1,000 values, mix them into 64 hidden features, apply a nonlinear GELU activation, and produce one polarization estimate. Each hidden unit can use every input bin. This is a useful first reference because its data flow is easy to follow.

Code the one-hidden-layer MLP

__init__ defines the layers and forward passes a batch through them. This is the model used by --architecture mlp.

import torch
from torch import nn

class BasicPolarizationMLP(nn.Module):
    def __init__(self):
        super().__init__()
        self.net = nn.Sequential(
            nn.Flatten(),
            nn.Linear(2 * 500, 64),
            nn.GELU(),
            nn.Linear(64, 1),
        )

    def forward(self, x):
        return self.net(x)

model = BasicPolarizationMLP()
print(model(torch.randn(8, 2, 500)).shape)  # torch.Size([8, 1])

The random input only checks the shape. A usable prediction requires training and the saved preprocessing.

2. Add a deeper dense DNN

Now use hidden layers of 256, 128, and 64 units. Successive nonlinear transformations can express more complicated relationships among baseline shape, amplitude, and spectral structure. Every layer still uses dense connections; the model must learn positional relationships from the examples.

Code the three-hidden-layer DNN

The input and output contract stays the same. This is --architecture dnn; the extra capacity is judged using the same validation rows.

import torch
from torch import nn

class DeepPolarizationDNN(nn.Module):
    def __init__(self):
        super().__init__()
        self.net = nn.Sequential(
            nn.Flatten(),
            nn.Linear(2 * 500, 256), nn.GELU(),
            nn.Linear(256, 128), nn.GELU(),
            nn.Linear(128, 64), nn.GELU(),
            nn.Linear(64, 1),
        )

    def forward(self, x):
        return self.net(x)

model = DeepPolarizationDNN()
print(model(torch.randn(8, 2, 500)).shape)  # torch.Size([8, 1])

More parameters do not guarantee a smaller error. The common training budget and saved learning curves make that tradeoff visible.

3. Use a multiscale CNN with physical summaries

Nearby frequency bins jointly describe peaks and shoulders. Convolution reuses a local filter across the sweep, while several filter widths and dilated residual blocks capture broader structure. Shared filters help with spectral shifts, although the complete estimator is not perfectly shift-invariant.

The 26 frozen coefficients are 25 summary weights plus one intercept. They are fitted once by ridge regression on the training rows. During subsequent neural-network training, those values stay fixed while the convolutional branch learns a correction. The model has 82,365 trainable parameters after this fit; its convolution filters remain trainable.

What does “shared filters” mean?

A filter forms a weighted sum of a short window spanning both input channels, adds a bias, then slides to the next frequency position. It uses the same weights at every position, so one learned local pattern can respond wherever it occurs in the sweep. Training updates those shared weights using evidence from all positions.

The first layer has three separate banks of 12 filters each, with widths 5, 15 and 31 bins. Each bank learns its own weights; sharing happens across frequency positions within each filter. One width-5 filter has 2 × 5 + 1 = 11 parameters, including its bias, reused across all 500 positions. All three banks together have 12 × (11 + 31 + 63) = 1,260 parameters and produce 36 feature channels. Zero padding preserves the frequency-axis length.

The wider filters combine more neighboring bins in their first step. Later dilated convolutions sample spaced positions to expand the context, and residual blocks add their input through a skip connection. Pooling and finite sweep boundaries mean that the complete prediction is not guaranteed to be unchanged by a spectral shift.

Optional stepping stone: code a compact 1D CNN

This smaller convolutional model shows how filters replace dense connections. It remains available as an additional control; the third model in our new comparison is the more capable multiscale architecture diagrammed below.

import torch
from torch import nn

class SmallPolarizationCNN(nn.Module):
    def __init__(self):
        super().__init__()
        self.encoder = nn.Sequential(
            nn.Conv1d(2, 16, kernel_size=7, padding=3),
            nn.GELU(),
            nn.AvgPool1d(2),
            nn.Conv1d(16, 32, kernel_size=7, padding=3),
            nn.GELU(),
            nn.AdaptiveAvgPool1d(1),
        )
        self.head = nn.Linear(32, 1)

    def forward(self, x):
        features = self.encoder(x).squeeze(-1)
        return self.head(features)

model = SmallPolarizationCNN()
example_batch = torch.randn(8, 2, 500)
print(model(example_batch).shape)  # torch.Size([8, 1])
print(sum(p.numel() for p in model.parameters()))

The numbers are untrained outputs. This model is available as --architecture compact. All four networks are defined in tools/nmr_lab.py; the factory build_model() selects the requested architecture.

The default PhysicsMultiscaleCNN combines learned filters with spectral summaries. The raw channel retains the voltage sweep. The second channel subtracts an independent reference and multiplies each bin by its trapezoidal df/f weight and TE calibration. Its sum is the conventional calibrated area estimate. Training-set standardization is then applied to both channels.

The two branches read the same standardized input. One computes fixed spectral summaries and applies a fitted linear estimate. The other learns convolutional features and a correction to that estimate. Their outputs are added, then multiplied by the saved target scale to return fractional polarization.

Which 25 physical summaries does the model use?

“Physical summaries” means numerical descriptors motivated by integrated signal, spectral shape and background level. They are calculated directly from the available input channels. The model does not fit a circuit or extract independent physical measurements inside this branch.

Let r be the standardized, TE-calibrated reference-subtracted channel and v the standardized raw channel. The coordinate u runs uniformly from −1 to +1 across the 500 bins. First locate an absolute-amplitude-weighted center and subtract it from that coordinate:

center = sum(abs(r) * u) / max(sum(abs(r)), 1e-6)
t = u - center

The zero-order moments retain overall signed and absolute level; higher powers weight contributions by distance from the center and encode aspects of shape and spread. These are amplitude-weighted moments of standardized channels, with no division by total signal area. They are not moments in MHz or a direct set of calibrated physical parameters. The standard deviations in this feature vector use the sample convention, using denominator 499 inside the square root; this differs from the population SD used to report prediction-error width.

The 25 summaries are each standardized again using their own training-row mean and population SD before entering the linear head. Near-zero summary SDs below 1e-8 are replaced by 1. The center and all 25 summaries are recomputed for every input; their formulas and saved scaling statistics stay fixed.

How are the 26 coefficients fitted and frozen?

A linear head assigns one weight to each of the 25 standardized summaries, then adds an intercept. Ridge regression chooses these 25 + 1 = 26 coefficients using the training labels, with a penalty that discourages large weights. In this implementation the objective is the sum of squared errors plus 0.001 × sum(weight²); the intercept is unpenalized.

linear estimate = intercept + sum(weight[k] * summary[k])
P_hat = target_scale * (linear estimate + CNN correction)

Both branches predict in normalized target units: the training labels are divided by the training-row population SD of P, with a floor of 1e-6. The final multiplication above restores fractional P. The dense correction head starts with a zero output, so training begins at the ridge estimate.

After the ridge fit, physics_head.requires_grad_(False) prevents gradient updates to its 25 weights and intercept. AdamW trains the remaining 82,365 parameters through the error of the combined prediction. Validation selects the checkpoint; validation and test labels never fit the ridge coefficients or scalers. At prediction time all saved weights and scaling statistics are reused.

The 26 values were learned from training data. “Frozen” describes their role during subsequent CNN training. The fixed frequency coordinate and saved summary means/SDs are buffers, stored with the model but excluded from the 82,391 parameter count. See PhysicsMultiscaleCNN and its physics_features(), calibrate() and forward() methods.

Keep physical information

Feature means and standard deviations are fitted on the training partition and reused later. Independently rescaling every spectrum to unit amplitude could erase the amplitude information needed to recover polarization.

Explain the source of the gain

This CNN combines shared filters with an explicit physical feature branch. Its accuracy measures both contributions together. Comparing it with the dense networks does not show how much of the gain comes from convolution alone.

Match the training objective to the scientific target

L = mean [ (P̂ − P) / strain ]²

The trainer uses mean squared error normalized by the training-label standard deviation strain. For a single P output, this constant rescaling preserves the absolute-MSE objective. A relative-error loss would weight small polarizations more strongly, which can conflict with an absolute-error target. Keep the training objective and the scientific acceptance criterion aligned.

Sampling and the loss jointly determine which errors receive attention. If low-|P| recovery is a priority, declare its tolerance and inspect that band during validation; a good pooled MSE may hide a poorly learned region. The current checkpoint selector uses pooled validation MSE. A different selection metric is a deliberate training change, not an effect of merely plotting more diagnostics.

Train long enough without chasing the training set

An epoch is one pass through the training data. A batch is the subset used for one optimizer update. The learning rate controls the step size. Our trainer uses AdamW with weight decay, dropout in the correction head, and a scheduler that reduces the learning rate when validation loss stalls.

For the walkthrough’s 1,200 training rows and default batch size 128, one epoch contains 10 updates, including a final smaller batch. Each update predicts the batch, computes scaled MSE, clears old gradients, backpropagates, clips the gradient norm at 5, and updates the trainable weights. Validation runs without gradient updates. The 26 ridge coefficients were already fitted on training rows and stay frozen. The epoch cap and early-stopping patience control how long this process runs; they do not add independent training examples.

Track both curves. Falling training loss with persistently rising validation loss suggests overfitting. If both remain high, consider underfitting, optimization trouble, or missing information. Early stopping limits training after a specified number of epochs without a new best validation value; it does not prove the model is correct. The trainer restores and saves the best validation checkpoint, not simply the last epoch.

Read the curves in context

The multiscale model uses dropout during training and disables it for validation, so training loss can exceed validation loss. The compact example does not use dropout. For the single-output absolute MSE used here, sqrt(val_loss) × target_scale converts validation loss back to RMSE in fractional P.

The trainer saves model.pt, scaler.npz, config.json, split.json, history.csv, metrics.json, and test_predictions.csv. The prediction CSV retains configuration IDs for group-based analysis. Keep these files together. This version saves at the end of a completed run; an interrupted run may leave no checkpoint.

How much training data does this network need?

There is no dependable “examples per weight” ratio. Neural weights, per-spectrum physical fit parameters, and training examples count different things. The multiscale model has 82,391 parameters: 26 are fitted ridge coefficients and 82,365 are optimized during neural training. A raw single-site fit has 12 physical/readout unknowns. Neither number converts directly into a required number of labeled spectra. The 500 bins in a sweep are features, not 500 independent training examples.

Parameter count describes part of model capacity; shared weights, regularization, the complexity of the forward response, noise and independent setup coverage also matter. Networks may have more parameters than training examples and still generalize under some conditions, or memorize without useful generalization. The parameter-count discussion and sources explain why a fixed multiplier is not a reliable planning rule.

Accuracy and precision need separate numbers

Define the residual as truth minus prediction: ei = Pi − P̂i. Bias measures a systematic offset; width measures the spread around that offset. In this tutorial we use bias to quantify systematic accuracy and residual width to quantify precision. Accuracy more broadly concerns closeness to truth, so also report total error.

This is the paper's Eq. (38) convention. Saved metrics use fractional P; multiply by 100 for percentage points.

Bias · systematic offset
b = mean(e)

A positive value means the model underestimates P on average.

Width · precision
σ = √mean[(e − b)²]

Residual standard deviation, not the standard error of the mean.

RMSE · total error
R = √mean(e²)

Combines offset and spread. It can be large for either reason.

RMSE² = bias² + width²

This empirical identity is exact when width uses ddof=0, as the tutorial helper does. It does not require Gaussian residuals. Inspect a histogram, the 95th percentile of absolute errors, and performance by polarization and noise level: one width can hide outliers and systematic trends.

Put the numbers on a polarization scale

Absolute metrics are in fractional P. Multiply by 100 to obtain percentage points (pp). To express an error relative to a chosen nonzero reference P₀, divide the fractional error by |P₀| and multiply by 100.

0.0005 fractional P = 0.05 pp
100 × 0.0005 / 0.05 = 1% relative error at P₀ = 5%

The same absolute error is 5% relative at P₀ = 1%, and 0.25% relative at P₀ = 20%. Relative error is undefined at zero. A denominator floor can stabilize a diagnostic, but the result depends on that convention.

From simulation to experiment

Recognize new structure.
Test predictions under shift.

At inference, an experimental spectrum may contain structure that the training data did not cover: a new shoulder, coherent ripple, a changed reference background or different noise correlations. The network still returns a polarization estimate, but its saved test error does not establish accuracy for those unfamiliar inputs. Detecting this change and measuring its effect on P is the practical purpose of studying input shift.

New experimental structure is the case to investigate

Consider a baseline ripple that was absent from the training examples and now appears in incoming spectra. After the saved preprocessing, it may change both the CNN's filter responses and its physical summaries. The fixed network can interpret some of that change as polarization. Shared filters and physical summaries alone do not establish robustness to it.

This illustrative scenario calls for three steps: identify the unfamiliar structure, compare it with training coverage, and test the resulting polarization error. An occasional unusual sweep calls for an individual input check; a recurring pattern or a change across acquisition periods calls for a distribution or temporal-drift check. These checks can begin before the physical cause or the true P of the incoming scans is known.

A measurement and validation protocol

This section describes the next scientific checks. The five butanol development scans and the separate UVA-ND3 example do not yet establish conditional noise/discrepancy matrices or experimental network accuracy. The 500-bin training and prediction exercises are runnable; their scores measure synthetic recovery. Independent experimental accuracy and the proposed conditional covariance/shift checks require their own evidence.

Retain systematic means alongside covariance so that a repeated new pattern remains visible.

Separate noise, baseline and signal using independent evidence

Write the full-fit residual as r = recorded − fitted. It can contain noise, imperfect baseline and signal models, and effects absorbed by fitted parameters. Subtracting the fitted baseline from the same sweep gives recorded − B̂ − Ŝ, which is still the original residual. It does not uniquely identify three separate errors.

What measurements separate the contributions?
  • Noise: use independent repeats of an unchanged signal and setup. For independent, equal-variance repeats, (scan1 − scan2)/√2 has single-sweep noise covariance. Check temporal correlation, tails and coherent pickup; common pickup can cancel in the difference.
  • Baseline: use corresponding signal-off references and characterize changes between reference and signal acquisition. A baseline reconstructed from the polarized fit is not an independent baseline measurement.
  • Signal: fit signal and electronics jointly, retaining their correlations and reference noise. Keep contributions that the data cannot separate explicitly unidentified.

For reference subtraction, retain Craw−ref = Craw + Cref − Craw,ref − Craw,refT. A reference shared across events also correlates their errors. The distinction between fitted-parameter uncertainty and model inadequacy is discussed by Kennedy and O'Hagan.

Retain systematic mismatch as well as covariance

Within a declared SNR, signed-P and setup group, retain the mean residual vector μr, the centered sample covariance Cr, and the second moment Mr. For N spectra on the same grid:

Mr = (1/N) Σi ririT
= [(N − 1)/N] Cr + μrμrT

An identical residual shape on every sweep has zero centered covariance but a nonzero systematic mismatch. Covariance alone misses that error. The bias-and-width lab illustrates the same distinction for polarization errors. Spectral residuals are in recorded units here; their covariance is in recorded-units squared, not fractional-P squared.

Propagate joint fit uncertainty without losing correlations

The covariance of fitted parameters is a different object from covariance across frequency bins. Under a locally linear, identifiable model with a justified noise covariance and no active bounds, propagate the full joint parameter covariance through derivatives of B and S. Keep the baseline–signal cross blocks:

Cfitted sweep = CBB + CSS + CBS + CSB

The correlated-input uncertainty rule retains these terms. Profiled readout coordinates belong in the joint uncertainty. The existing Jacobian condition numbers and spread among optimizer starts are not statistical covariance estimates. For nonlinear or bounded fits, use justified refit ensembles or profiles and retain failed fits and competing branches.

Fitting suppresses residual components that resemble parameter changes. Therefore post-fit residual covariance is not automatically the observation-noise covariance. Validate on independent acquisitions, and compare experimental and simulated residual diagnostics using the same fitting procedure. A small residual can coexist with biased fitted polarization.

Save sample counts, rank and any regularization. Five residual vectors yield centered covariance of rank at most four, even before subdividing by SNR or P. The supplied scans are not repeated observations at one unchanged setting. Subtracting noise and fit matrices from residual covariance does not automatically identify a physical discrepancy covariance.

Check whether incoming spectra are covered by training

Compare experimental and generated inputs on the same grid, in the same units, using the available raw/reference channels and frozen training preprocessing. Inspect the unfamiliar structure directly, then compare baseline and signal summaries, noise correlations, tails and joint feature coverage. For the multiscale CNN, changes to its 25 summaries and learned features can help locate what the estimator sees; retain raw-spectrum checks because a compressed summary can miss a new pattern.

For one incoming sweep, a predefined distance or anomaly score can flag departure from a training-derived reference. Set its threshold using separate development acquisitions and report its false-alarm behavior. Across multiple acquisitions or time windows, compare feature distributions, including their means and covariances; a predeclared two-sample diagnostic such as MMD can add evidence. Acquisitions are the samples; frequency bins are features. A single flagged sweep does not by itself establish a population shift.

A coverage flag identifies a reason to investigate; known-P evaluation determines the error. Preserve the flagged input, reference, timestamp and model version for inspection. Apply a response rule established during validation, such as routing unsupported inputs for review. Neither an unflagged input nor a plausible-looking P estimate guarantees accuracy. A larger input difference need not produce a larger P error: what matters is how the estimator responds to that particular structure.

How covariate shift, unfamiliar structure and temporal drift relate

Covariate shift describes a changed input distribution with the same conditional relationship p(P | input). Experimental inputs with new or underrepresented structure can fall into this case. Even when that relationship stays the same, a finite trained model may perform poorly in regions it has not learned adequately.

The new structure alone does not tell us whether the conditional relationship is preserved. Changes in detector response, material or the polarization population can also change it. Distribution shift covers both possibilities; temporal drift describes a change over time. This terminology helps choose an adaptation method. It does not postpone detecting unfamiliar inputs or testing their effect on P.

When experimental inputs occupy a region absent from training, the training data provide no direct check of the relationship there. Reweighting existing examples cannot supply missing examples of that structure. Additional characterization, physically supported generation or suitable new training data may be needed.

What can input covariance reveal about new structure?

Covariance describes how features vary together across acquisitions. A recurring pattern may change the mean, covariance or both; retain both and inspect the actual spectra. Matching these moments, or obtaining a nonsignificant two-sample test, does not establish that all relevant structure is covered. Record sample counts and sampling uncertainty.

The matrix difference Cexperiment − Ctraining is a useful comparison: some directions can gain variance while others lose it. It may therefore have negative eigenvalues. To simulate an added ripple or correlated noise, specify that process's mean, covariance and dependencies separately rather than using this difference as an additive-noise covariance.

Turn deviations into a measured polarization-error map

For the ripple example, construct paired nominal and disturbed inputs at the same simulator-known P, with a stated output disturbance supported by the experimental evidence. Vary its amplitude and pattern, run both inputs through the complete saved preprocessing and network, and measure the change in prediction as well as each prediction's error against truth. Use the physical forward model when changing electronics or material response; an arbitrary added nuclear peak is not automatically compatible with the original P label.

On an experimental sweep whose true P is unknown, a change in the network's output under a proposed perturbation measures sensitivity to that perturbation. Independent polarization evidence or justified known-P tests are needed to quantify accuracy. This separates detecting the new structure from measuring whether the estimator tolerates it.

Freeze the network and preprocessing. Evaluate known-P simulations under declared changes to noise amplitude/correlation, electronics, reference stability and physically supported signal shapes, including coupled changes beyond training coverage. Each cell of a robustness table should specify signed P × SNR × setup × disturbance mode/amplitude and report bias, width, RMSE, tails, tolerance-exceedance rate, independent-group counts and sampling uncertainty. Check interval coverage if intervals are supplied.

Define the scale and connect input mismatch to output error

The simulation convention is SNRpeak = max|signal| / max|noise|, excluding the baseline. An experimental max|fitted signal| / estimated noise SD is a different proxy and must be labeled separately. Do not estimate its denominator from structured full-fit RMS. Experimental fitted P is also an estimate, not an independent label; retain uncertainty when grouping scans. At P = 0 use absolute errors.

For a small input perturbation δw, let g be the sensitivity of the complete saved preprocessing-plus-network map to its raw/reference inputs. With error e = Ptrue − P̂, local propagation gives:

Δbias ≈ −gT μδw
Var(Δe) ≈ gT Cδw g

Use full propagation for large or nonlinear changes. Input residual RMS is not a polarization uncertainty. Do not add it in quadrature to test RMSE: units differ, dependencies matter, and the test error may already contain the same noise. Agreement with a fit used to construct the generator is not an independent accuracy test.

Temporal drift needs a separate chronological comparison with a validated reference period, alarm thresholds checked on stable periods, and independent polarization checks when available. Robustness tests establish performance for their specified disturbances; they cannot certify arbitrary unseen changes.

Broaden coverage, then test again

Correct supported model errors, then vary physically justified joint nuisance settings and noise/reference processes. Preserve the simulator's known P when constructing a stress case. Broader training can improve tolerance, but can also reduce precision or create ambiguities; extra variation cannot replace missing physics.

Reserve independent acquisitions before fitting discrepancy models or choosing augmentation ranges. Keep all descendants of a measured seed together, including source_scan_1based in the 500-bin workflow. After mitigation, test on untouched acquisitions and known-P stress cases. A full generator change requires new data and retraining before its results change.

Bring the three phases together

Your investigation.

Make one defensible improvement and explain the evidence.

  1. Write your measurement contract. State the P range, known calibration, expected noise and drift, absolute-error target, reference polarization, and latency budget.
  2. Audit the generator. Identify what would need matching to data, what varies, and what is still assumed. Compare at least two noise diagnostics.
  3. Run one controlled comparison. Compare the two CNNs on the same dataset and grouped split. Record settings and select using validation results. As a separate stress test, evaluate a fixed model on fresh data with correlated noise.
  4. Build an error budget. Report bias, residual width, RMSE, 95th-percentile absolute error, and event counts—pooled and in a signed P band. Convert to both pp and relative percent.
  5. Check the cost and limits. Report forward-pass and end-to-end timing on your hardware. Explain which unseen conditions your test does and does not cover.
Discussion: does generating ten times more data guarantee improvement?

No. Additional noise realizations help sample a fixed simulator distribution, but do not fix wrong physics, missing calibration, or uncovered configurations. Compare validation performance as you increase independent configuration coverage and event count separately. Keep the final evaluation frozen.

Discussion: how do you tell overfitting from missing information?

A widening training/validation gap suggests a generalization problem. Large errors on both can come from limited capacity, optimization, or an underdetermined input. Controlled tests—fixed calibration, reduced nuisance variation, or simulator-known quantities used only as diagnostics—help separate these explanations.
